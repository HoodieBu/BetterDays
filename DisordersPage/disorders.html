Understanding mental health conditions

What is anxiety?

Feeling anxious is a normal part of life. Many people worry about things such as health, money, school, work, or family. But anxiety disorders involve more than occasional worry or fear. For people with these disorders, anxiety does not go away, is felt in many situations, and can get worse over time.

There are several types of anxiety disorders, including:

  • Generalized anxiety disorder
  • Panic disorder
  • Social anxiety disorder
  • Various phobia-related disorders

What is depression?

Everyone feels sad or low from time to time, and those feelings usually fade on their own. Depression, sometimes called major depressive disorder or clinical depression, is different. It lasts longer and is more intense than everyday sadness.

Depression can cause serious symptoms that affect how you feel, think, and function in daily life. It can make activities like sleeping, eating, studying, or working much harder to manage.

This illness can affect anyone, no matter their age, background, income, culture, or education. Research suggests that a mix of genetic, biological, environmental, and psychological factors all contribute to the risk of developing depression.

What are the different types of depression?

The two most common forms of depression are major depression and persistent depressive disorder.

  • Major depression involves a depressed mood or loss of interest that interferes with daily life and lasts for at least two weeks.
  • Persistent depressive disorder (sometimes called dysthymia) has milder symptoms than major depression, but they last much longer—often two years or more.

Other types of depression can include:

  • Seasonal affective disorder, in which symptoms appear during certain seasons—most often in late fall and winter—and improve in spring and summer.
  • Bipolar disorder, sometimes called manic depression, which involves shifts between periods of depression and periods of elevated or irritable mood (mania or hypomania).
  • Depression with psychotic symptoms, a severe form of depression that also includes delusions (strongly held false beliefs) or hallucinations (seeing or hearing things that aren’t there).

What causes depression?

Depression doesn’t have a single cause. It often results from a combination of genetic, biological, environmental, and psychological factors. It can affect anyone at any age, but research shows it is diagnosed more often in women. Hormonal changes related to menstruation, pregnancy, the postpartum period, and menopause may play a role.

Other mental health conditions, ongoing stress, or chronic medical illnesses can also increase the risk of depression or make existing symptoms worse. Examples include long-term conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, or cancer, as well as difficult life experiences like trauma, loss, or major change.

What is PTSD?

People may have a range of reactions after experiencing or witnessing a traumatic event, such as a natural disaster, act of violence, or serious accident. These reactions often include feeling anxious, sad, or angry; having trouble concentrating or sleeping; and replaying what happened. For many people, these symptoms gradually improve over time.

However, when symptoms continue for an extended period and begin interfering with daily life—such as work, relationships, or routines—an individual may be diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). People with PTSD may continue to feel stressed or frightened even when they are safe.

What causes post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)?

Researchers do not fully understand why some people develop PTSD while others exposed to the same trauma do not. Genetics, brain chemistry, personal history, and individual risk factors may influence whether PTSD develops after a traumatic event.

Who is more likely to develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)?

PTSD can occur at any age. Several risk factors increase the likelihood of developing PTSD, including:

  • Your sex — women are more likely to develop PTSD.
  • Experiencing trauma during childhood.
  • Feeling intense fear, helplessness, or horror during a traumatic event.
  • Going through a trauma that lasts a long time.
  • Having little or no social support after the event.
  • Facing additional stress afterward, such as losing a loved one, home, or job.
  • A history of mental illness or substance use issues.

What are the symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)?

PTSD symptoms fall into four main categories. The way symptoms appear can vary from person to person.

  • Re-experiencing symptoms — when reminders of the trauma trigger distress or fear. Examples include:
    • Flashbacks, feeling as if the event is happening again
    • Nightmares
    • Frightening thoughts
  • Avoidance symptoms — avoiding people, places, or situations that are reminders of the traumatic event. This may involve:
    • Avoiding places or activities linked to the trauma
    • Avoiding thoughts, feelings, or conversations related to the event
  • Arousal and reactivity symptoms — symptoms that make someone feel tense, alert, or on edge. These can include:
    • Easily startled
    • Feeling tense or “on edge”
    • Difficulty sleeping
    • Angry outbursts
  • Cognition and mood symptoms — negative changes in thoughts or feelings, such as:
    • Trouble remembering important parts of the traumatic event
    • Negative beliefs about oneself or the world
    • Feeling guilt or blame
    • Losing interest in activities once enjoyed
    • Trouble concentrating

Symptoms may begin shortly after the traumatic event, but sometimes they do not appear until months or even years later. They can also fluctuate, improving at times and worsening at others.

If symptoms last longer than four weeks, cause serious distress, or interfere with work, school, or home life, a person may have PTSD and should seek professional support.

What is bipolar disorder?

Bipolar disorder (formerly called manic-depressive illness or manic depression) is a mental health condition that causes noticeable shifts in mood, energy, activity levels, and concentration. People with bipolar disorder experience periods of extremely “up,” energetic, or irritable behavior called manic episodes, and periods of “down,” sad, or hopeless behavior called depressive episodes.

What are the types of bipolar disorder?

There are three main types of bipolar disorder:

  • Bipolar I disorder involves manic episodes that last at least 7 days, or manic symptoms so severe that immediate hospital care is needed. Depressive episodes are also common and often last at least two weeks. Mixed episodes (feeling symptoms of both mania and depression) may also occur.
  • Bipolar II disorder involves depressive episodes along with episodes of hypomania, a milder form of mania. Hypomania is less severe than a full manic episode but still causes noticeable changes in energy and behavior.
  • Cyclothymic disorder (cyclothymia) involves repeated periods of hypomanic symptoms and depressive symptoms that are not as intense or long-lasting as true hypomanic or depressive episodes. Symptoms must last at least two years in adults or one year in children and teenagers.

With any form of bipolar disorder, having four or more episodes of mania or depression in a single year is known as rapid cycling.

What causes bipolar disorder?

The exact cause of bipolar disorder is unknown. However, several factors are thought to contribute, including genetics, differences in brain structure and function, and environmental influences such as stress or trauma.

Who is at risk for bipolar disorder?

You are at a higher risk of developing bipolar disorder if you have a close family member with the condition. Traumatic or highly stressful life events may also increase your risk.

What are the symptoms of bipolar disorder?

The symptoms of bipolar disorder vary from person to person, but they involve shifts in mood known as mood episodes, which may be manic, depressive, or a mix of both.

  • Symptoms of a manic episode may include:
    • Feeling very “up,” high, or elated
    • Feeling jumpy or wired; increased activity
    • Extreme irritability or a very short temper
    • Racing thoughts and unusually fast talking
    • Needing less sleep
    • Feeling unusually important, talented, or powerful
    • Engaging in risky behavior, such as spending large amounts of money, eating/drinking excessively, or unsafe sexual behavior
  • Symptoms of a depressive episode may include:
    • Feeling very sad, hopeless, or worthless
    • Feeling lonely or withdrawing from others
    • Talking very slowly or struggling to express thoughts
    • Having little or no energy
    • Sleeping too much or too little
    • Eating significantly more or less than usual
    • Loss of interest in activities once enjoyed
    • Difficulty focusing or making decisions
    • Thoughts of death or suicide
  • Symptoms of a mixed episode include both manic and depressive symptoms occurring together. This might mean feeling extremely energized while also feeling very sad, empty, or hopeless.

Some individuals experience milder symptoms. For example, hypomania may make someone feel very good or productive, without appearing obviously unwell. However, family and friends may still notice unusual changes in mood or behavior. Hypomania can be followed by depressive episodes.

Mood episodes may last a week or two, or sometimes longer. During an episode, symptoms typically occur every day for most of the day.

What is OCD?

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental health condition involving uncontrollable and recurring thoughts, images, or urges called obsessions, along with repetitive behaviors or mental rituals called compulsions. People with OCD feel driven to perform these behaviors in order to reduce anxiety or prevent something bad from happening, even when the behaviors are not logically connected to the fear.

OCD can begin at any age, but symptoms usually start between late childhood and early adulthood. Without treatment, OCD can worsen and interfere with work, school, and daily life.

What causes obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)?

The exact cause of OCD is unknown. However, genetics, brain chemistry, and environmental factors may all play a role in the development of the disorder.

Who is at risk for obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)?

OCD often begins during the teen or young adult years. Boys may develop OCD at a younger age than girls. Some factors that increase risk include:

  • Family history: Having a first-degree relative (parent, sibling, or child) with OCD increases risk.
  • Brain structure and functioning: Differences in certain brain areas have been observed in people with OCD.
  • Childhood trauma: Trauma early in life may contribute to the development of OCD.

In some cases, children may develop OCD or OCD-like symptoms following a streptococcal infection. This condition is called Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal Infections (PANDAS).

What are the symptoms of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD)?

People with OCD may experience obsessions, compulsions, or both. These symptoms can vary widely from person to person.

Obsessions

Obsessions are repeated, unwanted thoughts, urges, or mental images that cause anxiety. Common examples include:

  • Fear of germs or contamination
  • Fear of losing or misplacing things
  • Worries about harm coming to yourself or others
  • Unwanted forbidden thoughts involving sex or religion
  • Aggressive thoughts toward yourself or others
  • Needing things to be lined up exactly or arranged in a precise way

Compulsions

Compulsions are repetitive behaviors or mental acts that a person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession. These behaviors temporarily reduce anxiety but can become time-consuming. Common compulsions include:

  • Excessive cleaning or handwashing
  • Repeatedly checking things (locks, appliances, switches)
  • Compulsive counting
  • Arranging or ordering items in a very specific way

Some people with OCD also have Tourette syndrome or other tic disorders. Tics are sudden movements or sounds that are difficult to control.

What are eating disorders?

Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions marked by severe disturbances in a person’s eating behaviors. While many people may occasionally be concerned about their health, weight, or appearance, eating disorders involve persistent and harmful patterns.

In some cases, individuals become fixated on weight loss, body weight or shape, or controlling their food intake. These behaviors can negatively affect physical health, emotional well-being, and daily life, and may be signs of an eating disorder.

What are the types of eating disorders?

Common types of eating disorders include:

  • Binge-eating disorder involves out-of-control eating. People may continue eating even after they feel full and often eat until they are uncomfortably full. Afterwards, they may feel guilt, shame, or distress. Binge-eating disorder is the most common eating disorder in the United States and can lead to weight gain and obesity.
  • Bulimia nervosa involves cycles of binge-eating followed by behaviors meant to prevent weight gain, such as vomiting, using laxatives, fasting, or excessive exercise. People with bulimia may be underweight, normal weight, or overweight.
  • Anorexia nervosa involves avoiding food, severely restricting food intake, or eating very small amounts of certain foods. People with anorexia may see themselves as overweight even when they are dangerously underweight. Although it is less common, anorexia is often the most serious eating disorder and has the highest death rate of any mental illness.

What causes eating disorders?

The exact cause of eating disorders is unknown. Research suggests they result from a complex interaction of factors, including genetic, biological, behavioral, psychological, and social influences.

Who is at risk for eating disorders?

Anyone can develop an eating disorder, but they are more common in women. Eating disorders often begin during the teen years or young adulthood, although they can also develop during childhood or later in life.

What are the symptoms of eating disorders?

Symptoms of eating disorders vary depending on the specific disorder.

Symptoms of binge-eating disorder

  • Eating unusually large amounts of food in a short period of time
  • Eating even when not hungry or already full
  • Eating very quickly during binge episodes
  • Eating until uncomfortably full
  • Eating alone or in secret due to embarrassment
  • Feeling distressed, ashamed, or guilty about eating
  • Frequently dieting without weight loss

Symptoms of bulimia nervosa

Bulimia nervosa includes symptoms of binge-eating along with attempts to get rid of food or prevent weight gain, such as:

  • Purging by vomiting or using laxatives or enemas
  • Intense or excessive exercise
  • Fasting

Over time, bulimia nervosa can lead to serious health problems, including:

  • Chronically inflamed or sore throat
  • Swollen salivary glands in the neck and jaw
  • Worn tooth enamel and sensitive or decaying teeth
  • Acid reflux (GERD) and other gastrointestinal problems
  • Severe dehydration
  • Electrolyte imbalance, which can lead to stroke or heart attack

Symptoms of anorexia nervosa

  • Eating very little or starving oneself
  • Intense or excessive exercise
  • Extreme thinness
  • Strong fear of gaining weight
  • Distorted body image, seeing oneself as overweight despite being underweight

Over time, anorexia nervosa can cause severe health complications, including:

  • Bone thinning (osteopenia or osteoporosis)
  • Anemia
  • Muscle weakness and wasting
  • Thin, brittle hair and nails
  • Dry or yellowish skin
  • Growth of fine hair over the body
  • Severe constipation
  • Low blood pressure
  • Slowed breathing and pulse
  • Feeling cold constantly
  • Dizziness, fainting, or weakness
  • Extreme fatigue
  • Infertility
  • Damage to the heart
  • Brain damage
  • Multiple organ failure

Anorexia nervosa can be fatal. Some people die from complications of starvation, while others die from suicide.

Many people with eating disorders also experience other mental health conditions, such as depression or anxiety, or struggle with substance use.
