Panic Disorder: What You Need to Know

What is panic disorder?

People with panic disorder have frequent and unexpected panic attacks. These attacks are characterized by a sudden wave of fear or discomfort, or a sense of losing control—even when there is no clear danger or trigger. Not everyone who experiences a panic attack will develop panic disorder.

Panic attacks often include physical symptoms that can feel like a heart attack, such as trembling, tingling sensations, or a rapid heart rate. Panic attacks can occur at any time, sometimes even during sleep. Many people with panic disorder worry about having another attack and may change their lives significantly to try to prevent future attacks.

Panic disorder often begins in the late teens or early adulthood. Women are more likely than men to develop panic disorder. With the right treatment and support, people with panic disorder can manage symptoms and improve their quality of life.

What are the signs and symptoms of panic disorder?

People with panic disorder may have:

  • Sudden and repeated panic attacks of overwhelming anxiety and fear
  • A feeling of being out of control
  • A fear of death or impending doom during a panic attack
  • Intense worry about when the next panic attack will happen
  • Fear or avoidance of places where panic attacks happened in the past

Physical symptoms during a panic attack may include:

  • Pounding or racing heart
  • Sweating
  • Chills
  • Trembling
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Weakness or dizziness
  • Tingly or numb hands
  • Chest pain
  • Stomach pain or nausea

A panic attack can last from a few minutes to an hour, or sometimes longer. While panic attacks can feel extremely distressing, they are not life-threatening, and physical symptoms usually resolve over time. For some people, fear of future attacks can lead to ongoing anxiety.

What is the difference between a panic attack and panic disorder?

A panic attack is a one-time or occasional episode of intense fear with physical and emotional symptoms. An isolated panic attack is not a mental disorder.

Panic disorder may be diagnosed when a person has recurrent, unexpected panic attacks along with ongoing worry or behavior changes due to fear of future attacks. These patterns often interfere with daily life.

What causes panic disorder?

Panic disorder sometimes runs in families, but researchers do not fully understand why some people develop it while others do not. Studies suggest that certain brain areas and biological processes related to fear and anxiety may play a role.

Some researchers describe panic attacks as “false alarms,” where the body’s normal survival response activates too strongly or too often. For example, someone might feel a racing heart and interpret it as a heart attack, which can increase fear and trigger a cycle that leads to more panic attacks. Stress and environmental factors may also contribute.

How is panic disorder diagnosed?

To be diagnosed with panic disorder, a person must experience recurrent, unexpected panic attacks. They must also spend at least one month worrying about future attacks, fearing what the attacks mean, or changing behavior to avoid having another attack.

If you think you may be experiencing symptoms of panic disorder, talk to a health care provider such as a primary care doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist, or clinical social worker. A provider may ask when symptoms started, how long they have lasted, how often they occur, and how they affect your life. They may also do a physical exam to rule out other medical causes.

What other illnesses can co-occur with panic disorder?

Some people with panic disorder have other mental or physical health conditions, including depression, other anxiety disorders, PTSD, OCD, bipolar disorder, cardiovascular disease, respiratory disorders, irritable bowel syndrome, or thyroid problems. Panic disorder is also commonly linked with alcohol and substance misuse.

Agoraphobia can sometimes develop alongside panic disorder. People with agoraphobia have intense fear or anxiety about certain places or situations where escape or getting help might feel difficult if a panic attack occurs.

When multiple conditions are present, treatment can be more complex. A comprehensive plan that addresses the full picture of a person’s mental health is often most helpful.

How is panic disorder treated?

Treatment for panic disorder typically involves psychotherapy, medication, or both. The right approach depends on a person’s needs, preferences, and medical situation, and should be discussed with a mental health professional or health care provider. Finding the best plan can take time and may involve some trial and error.

Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy (also called talk therapy or counseling) can be effective in person or through telehealth. Some providers also recommend supportive tools like apps or other digital resources.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is commonly used to treat panic disorder. CBT teaches new ways of thinking, behaving, and reacting to the feelings that occur during or before a panic attack. Over time, attacks may become less frequent as a person learns to respond differently to physical sensations of fear.

Exposure therapy is a CBT method that helps a person gradually face fears connected to panic symptoms. Interoceptive exposure is another CBT technique that focuses on safely experiencing bodily sensations linked to panic (like increased heart rate) so they feel less frightening over time. These approaches may be combined with breathing techniques and relaxation exercises.

Medication

Health care providers may prescribe medication to treat panic disorder. Different types can be effective, including:

  • Antidepressants, such as SSRIs and SNRIs
  • Beta-blockers
  • Anti-anxiety medications, such as benzodiazepines

SSRIs and SNRIs are commonly used and may take several weeks to work. Some side effects can include headaches, nausea, or difficulty sleeping, and are often less severe when medication is started at a low dose and increased gradually.

Beta-blockers may help control physical symptoms such as rapid heart rate, sweating, and tremors in certain situations. Benzodiazepines can reduce panic symptoms quickly, but they may lead to tolerance or dependence, so providers typically prescribe them only for short periods.
