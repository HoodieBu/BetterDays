Social Anxiety Disorder: What You Need to Know

What is social anxiety disorder?

Social anxiety disorder is a common type of anxiety disorder. A person with social anxiety disorder experiences intense fear or anxiety in situations where they may be scrutinized, evaluated, or judged by others. These situations can include speaking in public, meeting new people, dating, job interviews, answering questions in class, asking for help, or talking to a cashier in a store.

Everyday activities such as eating or drinking in front of others or using a public restroom can also cause distress due to fear of embarrassment, judgment, or rejection. This fear often feels uncontrollable and can interfere with daily life.

People with social anxiety disorder may worry about upcoming social situations for weeks in advance and may avoid places or events that cause distress. In some cases, anxiety occurs primarily during performance situations, such as giving a speech, competing in sports, or performing music on stage.

Social anxiety disorder typically begins during childhood or adolescence and may resemble extreme shyness or avoidance of social situations. It occurs more frequently in women than men, particularly among adolescents and young adults. With the right treatment and support, people can manage symptoms and improve their quality of life.

What are the signs and symptoms of social anxiety disorder?

When interacting with or performing in front of others, people with social anxiety disorder may:

  • Blush, sweat, or tremble
  • Have a rapid heart rate
  • Feel their mind “go blank” or feel nauseous
  • Adopt a rigid posture or speak very softly
  • Avoid eye contact or struggle to speak in social situations
  • Feel intense self-consciousness or fear negative judgment
  • Avoid places or situations involving social interaction
  • Analyze their behavior afterward and focus on perceived flaws
  • Expect the worst possible outcomes from social situations

Children and teens with social anxiety disorder may also:

  • Avoid going to school
  • Have difficulty making friends
  • Complain of headaches or stomachaches
  • Have emotional outbursts in social situations
  • Refuse to speak or participate in group settings

Symptoms may change over time and are often worse during periods of stress or major life changes. Avoidance may offer short-term relief, but anxiety usually persists without treatment.

Is it normal stress or an anxiety disorder?

Everyone experiences stress from time to time, such as pressure from school, work, or major life changes. Stress is the body’s response to an external cause and may be temporary or ongoing.

Anxiety is the body’s reaction to stress and can occur even when there is no immediate threat. While occasional anxiety is normal, it becomes a concern when it does not go away and begins to interfere with daily life.

If anxiety persists and limits daily functioning, it may indicate an anxiety disorder rather than normal stress.

What causes social anxiety disorder?

Social anxiety disorder may run in families, though the exact cause is not fully understood. Research suggests that genetics, brain structure, and how the brain processes fear and anxiety all play a role.

Stressful life experiences and environmental factors may also contribute to the development of social anxiety disorder.

How is social anxiety disorder diagnosed?

To be diagnosed with social anxiety disorder, a person must experience strong fear or anxiety about social situations where they may be judged or scrutinized. These fears must last at least six months and significantly interfere with daily life.

A health care provider may ask about when symptoms started, how often they occur, and how they affect work, school, or relationships. A physical exam may be done to rule out other medical causes.

What other illnesses can co-occur with social anxiety disorder?

Social anxiety disorder often occurs alongside other conditions, such as depression, other anxiety disorders, OCD, ADHD, body dysmorphic disorder, or bipolar disorder. It is also commonly linked to alcohol or substance misuse.

People with social anxiety disorder may be less likely to seek treatment, and providers often identify it when treating another condition. Co-occurring disorders may require a more comprehensive treatment approach.

How is social anxiety disorder treated?

Treatment typically includes psychotherapy, medication, or a combination of both. Choosing the right approach depends on individual needs and preferences and may take time to find what works best.

Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy can be delivered in person or through telehealth and may be supported by digital tools. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the most widely used and well-studied treatment for social anxiety disorder.

CBT helps individuals change patterns of thinking and behavior that contribute to anxiety. It also helps people practice social skills in a supportive environment. Exposure therapy, a CBT technique, involves gradually facing feared social situations.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is another option that emphasizes mindfulness, acceptance of thoughts, and engagement in meaningful activities. Research continues to support its effectiveness for social anxiety disorder.

Medication

Medications such as SSRIs, SNRIs, beta-blockers, or anti-anxiety medications may be prescribed to help manage symptoms. A health care provider can help determine the most appropriate option.
