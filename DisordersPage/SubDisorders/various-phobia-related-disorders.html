Phobias: What You Need to Know

What is a phobia?

A phobia is an intense fear of—or aversion to—a specific object or situation. While anxiety is natural in certain circumstances, people with phobias experience fear that is out of proportion to the actual danger presented by the object or situation.

People with a phobia may:

  • Have an irrational or excessive worry about encountering the feared object or situation
  • Take active steps to avoid the feared object or situation
  • Experience immediate, intense anxiety when facing the feared object or situation
  • Endure unavoidable objects or situations with intense anxiety

What are the types of phobias and phobia-related disorders?

There are several types of phobias and phobia-related disorders, including:

Specific phobias

Specific phobias (sometimes called simple phobias) involve intense fear or anxiety about a particular object or situation. Common examples include fear of flying, fear of heights, fear of specific animals (such as spiders, dogs, or snakes), fear of receiving injections, and fear of blood.

Agoraphobia

People with agoraphobia have an intense fear of two or more of the following situations:

  • Using public transportation
  • Being in open spaces
  • Being in enclosed spaces
  • Standing in line or being in a crowd
  • Being outside the home alone

People with agoraphobia often avoid these situations because they worry that leaving might be difficult or that help might not be available if they experience panic-like reactions or other distressing symptoms. In severe cases, a person can become housebound.

Social anxiety disorder

Social anxiety disorder (previously called social phobia) involves an intense, persistent fear of being watched and judged by others. (You can also view the Social Anxiety Disorder page for more details.)

What causes phobia-related disorders?

Research suggests that both genetic and environmental factors play a role in developing phobias. Some risk factors include:

  • Exposure to a traumatic event involving a specific object or situation
  • Feeling distressed or nervous in new situations during childhood
  • A family history of anxiety or other mental disorders

How are phobia-related disorders treated?

Psychotherapy is the primary treatment for phobias. Medications may help in certain situations depending on a person’s symptoms and needs.

Psychotherapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a well-established and effective treatment for anxiety disorders such as phobias. CBT helps people change patterns of thinking and behavior that keep fear going and teaches strategies to reduce anxiety.

Exposure therapy is a CBT method that is especially effective for phobias. It focuses on gradually confronting the fears underlying a phobia so a person can engage in situations they have been avoiding. Exposure therapy may also be paired with relaxation exercises.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) is another option for some anxiety disorders, including phobias. ACT uses strategies like mindfulness and goal setting to reduce discomfort and anxiety. Compared with CBT, ACT is newer, so less research is available on its effectiveness.

Medication

A health care provider may prescribe medications such as anti-anxiety medications or beta-blockers to help relieve symptoms in specific situations. A provider can help evaluate whether medication should be part of a treatment plan.
